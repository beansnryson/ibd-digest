Pain and Sedative Prescribing and Diagnostic Delay in Inflammatory Bowel Disease: A National Population-Based Cohort Study

Baillie S, Saxena S, Petersen I, Bottle A, et al.

IBDOriginal Research
Show abstract

INTRODUCTION: Symptoms of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) often evolve over 5 years before diagnosis, yet little is known about pain and sedative medication use during this period. We aimed to characterise prescribing trajectories before and after IBD diagnosis and examine associations of prescribing with diagnostic delays.

METHODS: Using UK Clinical Practice Research Datalink-GOLD, we identified adults with IBD diagnosed between 2010 and 2021. Among those with 5 years' follow-up before and after diagnosis, we assessed prescribing prevalences of opioids, gabapentinoids, tricyclic antidepressants, benzodiazepines, and Z-drugs. Among those with 5 years' follow-up before diagnosis, Cox regression examined associations between prescribing and time from first gastrointestinal symptom to diagnosis. Time-to-discontinuation of long-term prescriptions after diagnosis was examined in those with follow-up 1 year before diagnosis and 5 years after.

RESULTS: Among 4433 adults with IBD, the proportion prescribed pain or sedative medications doubled from 8% to 16% during the 5 years before diagnosis and remained high (19% to 21%) throughout the subsequent 5 years. In a second cohort of 8848 individuals, median time from first gastrointestinal symptom to diagnosis was 13 months longer for UC and 14 months longer for CD among those prescribed pain or sedative medications than those who were not. Median time to discontinuation of long-term prescriptions after diagnosis was 4-8 months.

CONCLUSIONS: Pain and sedative prescribing is common before and after IBD diagnosis and is associated with a longer time to diagnosis. Timely investigation for IBD should be considered in patients receiving these medications who present with suggestive symptoms.

Aliment Pharmacol Ther Nov 27 2026

Predictors of Response to Anti-Tumour Necrosis Factor Treatment in Inflammatory Bowel Disease: A Nationwide Danish Cohort Study

Lie M, Vestergaard MV, Asaad AK, Larsen L, et al.

IBDOriginal ResearchAnti-TNF
Show abstract

BACKGROUND: Despite recent progress in advanced therapies, treatment with an anti-tumour necrosis factor (anti-TNF) drug remains a cornerstone of therapy in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD). However, 13%-40% of patients experience primary non-response (PNR) and around 50% a secondary loss of response (LOR). No predictors of treatment response have yet been sufficient for clinical use, often due to small cohort sizes.

AIMS: In this study, we aimed to analyse associations with and predictors of response to anti-TNF therapy in patients with IBD utilizing Danish nationwide registry-based data.

METHODS: All biologic-naïve patients with IBD in Denmark who completed infliximab or adalimumab induction were included, totalling 14,072 patients. Events considered indicative of PNR or LOR were prescription of systemic corticosteroids, major IBD-related surgery or hospitalization, unsatisfactory reduction in CRP, intensification of treatment, or a different biologic. Associations tested included demographics, biochemical samples, concurrent medication, severity and behaviour of IBD.

RESULTS: In total, 19.0% of patients experienced PNR and 43.2% LOR. We found an increased hazard of LOR with longer disease duration before treatment, a protective effect of immunomodulators during induction, and associations with baseline biochemical samples. Finally, we present moderately predictive models of PNR to infliximab (mean AUC 0.71) and adalimumab (mean AUC 0.66).

CONCLUSIONS: In this population-based cohort of over 14,000 patients with IBD, we present novel baseline biochemical associations with response to anti-TNFs, prediction models for PNR to infliximab and adalimumab, and show findings in support of the shift from a bottom-up to a top-down approach in treating IBD.

Type 2 diabetes mellitus is not associated with risk of incident inflammatory bowel disease in U.S. adults

Saito M, Lopes EW, Casey K, Tracy M, et al.

Crohn's DiseaseUlcerative ColitisOriginal Research
Show abstract

BACKGROUND: Some observational studies have linked higher dietary sugar intake to increased risk of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), though few studies have evaluated diabetes and IBD risk. Here, we examined the relationship between type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and incident IBD.

METHODS: We utilized data comprising 225 328 U.S. adults (mean baseline age 46 years) from the prospective Nurses' Health Study (1986-2020), Nurses' Health Study II (1991-2021), and Health Professionals Follow-Up Study (1986-2020). We excluded those with IBD at baseline and type 1 diabetes mellitus. We confirmed T2DM via validated questionnaires and IBD through medical record review. Using Cox proportional hazards models, we estimated hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for Crohn's disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC) according to diagnosis of T2DM, adjusting for confounders. We also examined hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) and metformin use and IBD risk.

RESULTS: We documented 391 CD and 510 UC cases over 6 171 602 person-years of follow-up. T2DM was not associated with risk of CD (P = .28) or UC (P = .79). Compared with participants without T2DM, those with T2DM had an adjusted hazard ratio of 0.75 (95% CI, 0.44-1.27) for CD and 1.06 (95% CI, 0.69-1.62) for UC. In participants with available HbA1c (n = 5900), there was no association between having at least prediabetes (HbA1c  ≥5.7) and risk IBD (all P ≥ .32). Metformin use was also not associated with IBD risk (all P ≥ .15).

CONCLUSIONS: T2DM was not associated with incident CD or UC, indicating that IBD risk is not influenced by insulin resistance. Other potential mechanisms to explain the increased risk of IBD seen with dietary sugar intake should be the focus of future study.

Narrow-band imaging vs artificial intelligence for colonic surveillance in inflammatory bowel disease: a prospective, crossover study. Results from the CLEAR-IBD (colorectal lesion evaluation assisted by real-time AI in inflammatory bowel disease) trial

Serra-Ruiz X, Pigrau M, Castillo Cejas MD, Céspedes-Martínez E, et al.

IBDOriginal Research
Show abstract

BACKGROUND: Artificial intelligence (AI)-based computer-aided detection (CADe) systems are increasingly used in colonoscopy but are trained mainly in non-inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) populations. The performance of AI-based CADe for detecting colitis-associated dysplasia compared with established imaging techniques remains unclear.

OBJECTIVE: This study compared colorectal dysplasia miss rates (DMR) between a non-IBD-trained CADe system (ENDO-AID CADe, Olympus) and narrow-band imaging (NBI) in patients with IBD undergoing surveillance colonoscopy.

METHODS: We conducted a unicentric, open-label, quasi-randomized crossover trial including IBD patients undergoing colorectal cancer surveillance. Each patient underwent colonoscopy with both CADe and NBI in alternating order. The primary outcome was the per-lesion DMR; a per-patient analysis was also performed. Sample size was pragmatically set at 60 patients based on previously published crossover studies comparing chromoendoscopy and NBI in this population.

RESULTS: Sixty patients were included. In the per-lesion analysis, DMR was 42.9% (6/14) for NBI and 23.8% (5/21) for CADe (risk difference 19.0%; 95% CI, -11.2 to 46.9; P = .28). In the per-patient analysis, DMR was 16.7% (1/6) for NBI and 22.2% (2/9) for CADe (risk difference -5.6%; 95% CI, -40.8 to 37.2; P = 1.00). CADe detected more lesions overall than NBI (125 vs 75; P = .006), but a higher proportion were inflammatory (53.6% vs 13.3%; P < .001). NBI detected more hyperplastic lesions (66.7% vs 28%; P < .001).

CONCLUSIONS: Dysplasia miss rates did not differ significantly between CADe and NBI, although wide CIs limit definitive conclusions and findings should be considered hypothesis generating. These results support further evaluation of AI as an adjunct to established endoscopic imaging, while highlighting the need for AI models specifically trained in IBD populations.

Timing of exposure to a Western-type diet influences the development of chronic intestinal inflammation in IL-10-deficient mice

Luchak A, Ren MY, Streutker CJ, Bayer G, et al.

Crohn's DiseaseOriginal Research
Show abstract

BACKGROUND: Western-style dietary patterns are associated with an increased risk for the development of inflammatory bowel disease. However, the relative impact of consuming a Western diet in early life on susceptibility to chronic intestinal inflammation is not known. Using the interleukin (IL)-10-deficient murine model of spontaneous colitis, we aimed to determine the effect of diet timing on disease course, T cell responses and gut microbiome composition.

METHODS: A Western-type diet (WD) was fed to conventional IL-10-deficient mice either in early life or in late adolescence. Colitis progression was characterized by antimicrobial peptide levels, gene expression patterns, and histopathological features. Gut-related lymphocyte populations were phenotyped by flow cytometry and fecal microbiome composition was determined by 16S rRNA sequencing.

RESULTS: WD feeding in early life but not in late adolescence led to an accelerated onset of colitis in IL-10-deficient mice. Both prior to and during disease, early life WD-fed mice presented with an altered pool of regulatory T cells and T helper subsets, in line with the early life WD feeding window overlapping with the establishment of colon memory CD4 T cell populations. A few long-term changes in the fecal microbiome correlated with colitis severity; the most prominent of which, an increase in Flavonifractor plautii, was also related to Crohn's disease risk in a predisease cohort.

CONCLUSIONS: Exposure to dietary risk factors in early life can have long-term effects on the gut immune-microbial landscape and disease susceptibility. Future studies investigating the effect of dietary components on inflammatory bowel disease risk should prioritize the inclusion of early age groups.

Time to Flip the Script: Advanced Therapy Is Appropriate for Most Patients with Recently Diagnosed IBD

Mecsas-Faxon BD, Cheifetz AS, Barnes EL, Rai V, et al.

Crohn's DiseaseUlcerative ColitisOriginal Research
Show abstract

Guidelines recommend initiating advanced therapy in patients with moderate-to-severe inflammatory bowel disease (IBD). Although early use of advanced therapy may be beneficial in slowing or halting disease progression, there is limited clinical trial and real-world evidence defining appropriateness of starting advanced therapy within 1 year of diagnosis. To address this gap, 11 IBD specialists defined 144 hypothetical scenarios representative of patients seen in clinical practice and rated them as inappropriate, uncertain, or appropriate for advanced therapy initiation using the RAND Appropriateness Method. We considered key variables likely to drive treatment decisions in this first year, including endoscopic disease severity, degree of symptoms, prior mesalamine in ulcerative colitis (UC), disease extent in Crohn's disease (CD), prior systemic corticosteroids, and age. Scenarios were rated independently, then re-rated via an anonymous survey after discussion at an in-person meeting. Overall, our expert panel identified few clinical scenarios in which it would be inappropriate to initiate advanced therapy within 1 year of IBD diagnosis. For patients with UC, panelists rated it appropriate to start advanced therapy in those with Mayo 3 disease and inappropriate in nearly all patients with Mayo 1 disease and no prior use of mesalamine. For patients with CD, panelists rated advanced therapy appropriate for all patients with deep ulcers, and nearly all with shallow ulcers or erosions who are symptomatic. Scenarios rated uncertain included asymptomatic patients with UC Mayo 2 not previously treated with mesalamine, as well as all asymptomatic patients with CD erosions. Our findings support a role for early advanced therapy in most patients within 1 year of IBD diagnosis and underscore the importance of individualizing treatment decisions in IBD.

Efficacy and Safety of Mirikizumab After Switching From Ustekinumab in Crohn's Disease: Results From the VIVID-2 Extension Study

Sands BE, D'Haens G, Dulai PS, Laharie D, et al.

Crohn's DiseaseOriginal ResearchAnti-IL-12/23Anti-IL-23
Show abstract

INTRODUCTION: Mirikizumab, an anti-interleukin-23p19 monoclonal antibody, was safe and efficacious in patients with moderately-to-severely active Crohn's disease (CD) in the placebo- and active-controlled (ustekinumab) phase 3 VIVID-1 study. Here we report efficacy and safety for ustekinumab endoscopic non-responders from VIVID-1 who switched to open-label mirikizumab in VIVID-2.

METHODS: Of 109 ustekinumab endoscopic non-responders, 102 were included in the safety population and 92 with a Simple Endoscopic Score for CD ≥7 (or ≥4 for isolated ileal disease) at the VIVID-1 baseline formed the efficacy population for this analysis. Ustekinumab endoscopic non-responders received intravenous mirikizumab 900 mg at weeks 0, 4, and 8 of VIVID-2, then subcutaneous mirikizumab 300 mg every 4 weeks. Outcomes included endoscopic response and remission, CD Activity Index (CDAI) remission, corticosteroid-free CDAI remission, inflammatory biomarkers, and safety through week 52 of VIVID-2. Modified non-responder imputation (mNRI) and observed case (OC) analyses are reported.

RESULTS: At week 52 of VIVID-2, 41.4%/46.2% (mNRI/OC) of ustekinumab endoscopic non-responders achieved endoscopic response, 22.0%/24.4% achieved endoscopic remission, 75.0%/84.8% achieved CDAI remission, and 75.0%/84.8% achieved corticosteroid-free CDAI remission with 1 year of mirikizumab treatment. Median C-reactive protein and fecal calprotectin decreased (from 6.1 to 3.4 mg/L and 1169.0 to 216 µg/g, respectively) by week 52 of VIVID-2. Over 1 year of mirikizumab treatment, 64.7% experienced treatment-emergent adverse events, and 6.9% had serious adverse events.

DISCUSSION: Mirikizumab was efficacious in patients with moderately-to-severely active CD who failed to achieve endoscopic response with ustekinumab. Safety was generally consistent with the known mirikizumab safety profile.
