Timing of exposure to a Western-type diet influences the development of chronic intestinal inflammation in IL-10-deficient mice

Luchak A, Ren MY, Streutker CJ, Bayer G, et al.

Crohn's DiseaseOriginal Research
Show abstract

BACKGROUND: Western-style dietary patterns are associated with an increased risk for the development of inflammatory bowel disease. However, the relative impact of consuming a Western diet in early life on susceptibility to chronic intestinal inflammation is not known. Using the interleukin (IL)-10-deficient murine model of spontaneous colitis, we aimed to determine the effect of diet timing on disease course, T cell responses and gut microbiome composition.

METHODS: A Western-type diet (WD) was fed to conventional IL-10-deficient mice either in early life or in late adolescence. Colitis progression was characterized by antimicrobial peptide levels, gene expression patterns, and histopathological features. Gut-related lymphocyte populations were phenotyped by flow cytometry and fecal microbiome composition was determined by 16S rRNA sequencing.

RESULTS: WD feeding in early life but not in late adolescence led to an accelerated onset of colitis in IL-10-deficient mice. Both prior to and during disease, early life WD-fed mice presented with an altered pool of regulatory T cells and T helper subsets, in line with the early life WD feeding window overlapping with the establishment of colon memory CD4 T cell populations. A few long-term changes in the fecal microbiome correlated with colitis severity; the most prominent of which, an increase in Flavonifractor plautii, was also related to Crohn's disease risk in a predisease cohort.

CONCLUSIONS: Exposure to dietary risk factors in early life can have long-term effects on the gut immune-microbial landscape and disease susceptibility. Future studies investigating the effect of dietary components on inflammatory bowel disease risk should prioritize the inclusion of early age groups.

Time to Flip the Script: Advanced Therapy Is Appropriate for Most Patients with Recently Diagnosed IBD

Mecsas-Faxon BD, Cheifetz AS, Barnes EL, Rai V, et al.

Crohn's DiseaseUlcerative ColitisOriginal Research
Show abstract

Guidelines recommend initiating advanced therapy in patients with moderate-to-severe inflammatory bowel disease (IBD). Although early use of advanced therapy may be beneficial in slowing or halting disease progression, there is limited clinical trial and real-world evidence defining appropriateness of starting advanced therapy within 1 year of diagnosis. To address this gap, 11 IBD specialists defined 144 hypothetical scenarios representative of patients seen in clinical practice and rated them as inappropriate, uncertain, or appropriate for advanced therapy initiation using the RAND Appropriateness Method. We considered key variables likely to drive treatment decisions in this first year, including endoscopic disease severity, degree of symptoms, prior mesalamine in ulcerative colitis (UC), disease extent in Crohn's disease (CD), prior systemic corticosteroids, and age. Scenarios were rated independently, then re-rated via an anonymous survey after discussion at an in-person meeting. Overall, our expert panel identified few clinical scenarios in which it would be inappropriate to initiate advanced therapy within 1 year of IBD diagnosis. For patients with UC, panelists rated it appropriate to start advanced therapy in those with Mayo 3 disease and inappropriate in nearly all patients with Mayo 1 disease and no prior use of mesalamine. For patients with CD, panelists rated advanced therapy appropriate for all patients with deep ulcers, and nearly all with shallow ulcers or erosions who are symptomatic. Scenarios rated uncertain included asymptomatic patients with UC Mayo 2 not previously treated with mesalamine, as well as all asymptomatic patients with CD erosions. Our findings support a role for early advanced therapy in most patients within 1 year of IBD diagnosis and underscore the importance of individualizing treatment decisions in IBD.

Efficacy and Safety of Mirikizumab After Switching From Ustekinumab in Crohn's Disease: Results From the VIVID-2 Extension Study

Sands BE, D'Haens G, Dulai PS, Laharie D, et al.

Crohn's DiseaseOriginal ResearchAnti-IL-12/23Anti-IL-23
Show abstract

INTRODUCTION: Mirikizumab, an anti-interleukin-23p19 monoclonal antibody, was safe and efficacious in patients with moderately-to-severely active Crohn's disease (CD) in the placebo- and active-controlled (ustekinumab) phase 3 VIVID-1 study. Here we report efficacy and safety for ustekinumab endoscopic non-responders from VIVID-1 who switched to open-label mirikizumab in VIVID-2.

METHODS: Of 109 ustekinumab endoscopic non-responders, 102 were included in the safety population and 92 with a Simple Endoscopic Score for CD ≥7 (or ≥4 for isolated ileal disease) at the VIVID-1 baseline formed the efficacy population for this analysis. Ustekinumab endoscopic non-responders received intravenous mirikizumab 900 mg at weeks 0, 4, and 8 of VIVID-2, then subcutaneous mirikizumab 300 mg every 4 weeks. Outcomes included endoscopic response and remission, CD Activity Index (CDAI) remission, corticosteroid-free CDAI remission, inflammatory biomarkers, and safety through week 52 of VIVID-2. Modified non-responder imputation (mNRI) and observed case (OC) analyses are reported.

RESULTS: At week 52 of VIVID-2, 41.4%/46.2% (mNRI/OC) of ustekinumab endoscopic non-responders achieved endoscopic response, 22.0%/24.4% achieved endoscopic remission, 75.0%/84.8% achieved CDAI remission, and 75.0%/84.8% achieved corticosteroid-free CDAI remission with 1 year of mirikizumab treatment. Median C-reactive protein and fecal calprotectin decreased (from 6.1 to 3.4 mg/L and 1169.0 to 216 µg/g, respectively) by week 52 of VIVID-2. Over 1 year of mirikizumab treatment, 64.7% experienced treatment-emergent adverse events, and 6.9% had serious adverse events.

DISCUSSION: Mirikizumab was efficacious in patients with moderately-to-severely active CD who failed to achieve endoscopic response with ustekinumab. Safety was generally consistent with the known mirikizumab safety profile.

Long-Term Real-World Switch Rates and Adherence Among Patients With Ulcerative Colitis Treated With Upadacitinib or Tumour Necrosis Factor Inhibitors

Siegel CA, Ungaro RC, Kim JR, Shukla N, et al.

Ulcerative ColitisOriginal ResearchAnti-TNFJAK inhibitor
Show abstract

BACKGROUND: Understanding long-term differences in real-world treatment patterns in patients with ulcerative colitis treated with advanced therapies may provide insight into treatment effectiveness and patient preference.

AIMS: To evaluate real-world treatment patterns over 24 months including treatment switching and adherence (overall and advanced-therapy-experienced populations), in patients with ulcerative colitis initiating upadacitinib compared with those initiating adalimumab or infliximab.

METHODS: This was a retrospective, cohort study using administrative claims data from the Merative MarketScan US commercial and Medicare databases (January 2022-October 2025). Adults with ulcerative colitis initiating treatment with upadacitinib, adalimumab, or infliximab were included and evaluated for differences in switching and adherence over 24 months after the index date. Multivariable regression analyses were conducted to control for confounders.

RESULTS: 946 patients with ulcerative colitis were included; upadacitinib (n = 164; [17.3%]), adalimumab (n = 475; [50.2%]) and infliximab (n = 307 [32.5%]). At 24 months, upadacitinib had the lowest rate of switching to a new advanced therapy (20.1%) versus infliximab (49.2%) or adalimumab (49.7%); adjusted odds of switching were significantly higher with other therapies compared with upadacitinib (all p < 0.001). Upadacitinib had the highest adherence at 24 months (68.3%) versus adalimumab (40.0%) or infliximab (42.3%). Similar trends were observed in the advanced-therapy-experienced population and after adjusting for differences in baseline characteristics.

CONCLUSIONS: In this real-world claims-based analysis of patients with ulcerative colitis, treatment with upadacitinib was associated with the lowest switch rate and highest adherence rate when compared with adalimumab or infliximab.

A hydrogen peroxide-tolerant Bifidobacterium longum strain improves colitis by resisting oxidative stress

Kim JH, Kim SW, Shin Y, Kim KB, et al.

IBDOriginal Research
Show abstract

IMPORTANCE: Inflammation-associated oxidative stress can limit the viability and efficacy of obligate anaerobic probiotics such as Bifidobacterium. Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is characterized by sustained intestinal inflammation accompanied by elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS), creating an unfavorable niche for oxygen-sensitive commensals.

OBJECTIVE: To evaluate the antioxidant, epithelial barrier-protective, and therapeutic potential of a hydrogen peroxide (H2O2)-resistant Bifidobacterium longum strain (S2).

DESIGN: Experimental study involving in vitro functional assays, whole-genome sequencing, and three murine colitis models.

SETTING: Laboratory-based cell culture and animal experiments.

PARTICIPANTS: A bacterial strain isolated from healthy adult feces and mice with experimentally induced colitis.

INTERVENTIONS OR EXPOSURES: Oral administration of S2, compared with vehicle or the reference strain ATCC 15697.

MAIN OUTCOMES AND MEASURES: H2O2 tolerance, antioxidant activity, epithelial permeability, aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) activity, fecal recovery, survival, clinical indices, histopathology, and mucus barrier markers.

RESULTS: In vitro, S2 was significantly less inhibited by H2O2 and exhibited stronger radical-scavenging activity. S2 also reduced TNF-α- or LPS-induced epithelial permeability and increased aryl hydrocarbon receptor reporter activity. Whole-genome sequencing (2 365 469 bp; 59.94% G + C) placed S2 within the B. longum species and revealed genetic divergence from the H2O2-sensitive reference strain ATCC 15697. Consistent with robust survival under H2O2 exposure, genomic analysis showed a conserved thioredoxin/AhpC-centered peroxide detoxification system with additional redox-active enzymes. In vivo, oral administration of S2 resulted in higher fecal recovery than ATCC 15697, and viable recovery remained increased in RAG1-deficient mice, indicating that S2 persistence is not solely dependent on adaptive immunity. Importantly, across DSS, DNBS, and T-cell transfer colitis models, oral gavage of S2 significantly improved the survival rate, clinical indices, and histopathology while preserving goblet cells and mucin and increasing barrier-associated gene expression.

CONCLUSIONS AND RELEVANCE: Collectively, these results identified B. longum S2 as an oxidative stress-tolerant probiotic candidate with barrier-protective and immunomodulatory effects, supporting a strategy of selecting oxidative stress-tolerant bifidobacterial strains to improve probiotic performance in ROS-rich intestinal inflammation such as IBD.

Beyond cytokines in inflammatory bowel disease: targeting the epithelial microbiome-mitochondria-immune axis to restore metabolic homeostasis

Torossian T, Choufany E, Bazzi S, Chahine A, et al.

IBDOriginal Research
Show abstract

Inflammatory bowel disease is a chronic inflammatory disease characterized by mucosal immunity dysregulation, epithelial barrier dysfunction, and gut microbiome dysbiosis. While cytokine-targeted therapies have improved clinical outcomes, many patients remain refractory or relapse, emphasizing the need for additional therapeutic approaches. Recent evidence suggests that mitochondrial metabolism in the intestinal epithelium regulates mucosal homeostasis, immune cell recruitment, and susceptibility to inflammation. However, it remains controversial whether mitochondrial dysfunction is a cause or a consequence of intestinal inflammation. Epithelial energetic deficiency and barrier dysfunction are associated with impaired oxidative phosphorylation, defective short-chain fatty acid utilization, redox imbalance, and faulty mitochondrial quality control. Indeed, impaired mitophagy promotes mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) release through voltage-dependent anion channel 1 (VDAC1) oligomerization and mitochondrial permeability transition pore opening. Extracellular mtDNA acts as a damage-associated molecular pattern, activating cyclic GMP-AMP synthase-stimulator of interferon genes, toll-like receptor 9, and NLR family pyrin domain-containing 3 inflammasome pathways that amplify mucosal inflammation. These metabolic changes alter luminal oxygen and microbiome composition, whereas microbial metabolites, particularly tryptophan-derived indoles and kynurenines, modulate epithelial mitochondrial function through aryl hydrocarbon receptor signaling. These findings support a bidirectional microbiome-mitochondria-immune axis that potentiates intestinal inflammation. In this review, we summarize the current evidence of how epithelial mitochondrial dysfunction associates with intestinal inflammation. We discuss how mitochondrial metabolism interfaces with microbial ecology and immune regulation to shape disease pathogenesis. We also discuss therapeutic strategies targeting this axis: mitochondrial-targeted antioxidants (eg, MitoQ), VDAC1 inhibitors, bile-acid derivatives (eg, tauroursodeoxycholic acid), and metabolite-based therapies to restore microbial tryptophan metabolism. These approaches target mitochondrial bioenergetics and microbial metabolic cross-talk with the potential to restore microbiome-immune homeostasis, preserve barrier integrity, and complement conventional immune-directed therapies.
